DIY lab: build a contact microphone

A contact microphone picks up vibrations moving through a solid surface. It can help reveal tiny taps and scrapes that an ordinary air microphone may miss. This guide is a beginner-friendly prototype, not a calibrated insect detector.

What you need

  • One piezo disc sensor (about 20–35 mm)
  • Two-conductor shielded cable
  • A compatible high-impedance audio input, recorder, or piezo preamp
  • Insulating tape or heat-shrink tubing and a small plastic enclosure
  • A soft elastic band or removable putty to hold the sensor against a surface

For the easiest setup, use a ready-made piezo contact microphone or a piezo preamp with a suitable cable. Input connectors vary; check the recorder/interface instructions before connecting bare wires.

Build and test

  1. Connect the piezo disc’s two terminals to the cable’s signal conductor and ground/shield, following the sensor and input-device instructions.
  2. Insulate each connection so the terminals cannot touch each other, then secure the disc and cable in a small enclosure to reduce strain.
  3. Connect it to a compatible high-impedance input. Start with the input gain low and raise it gradually.
  4. Gently press the sensor against a dry twig, fallen leaf, or wooden surface. Tap the surface lightly and listen through headphones.

Try this listening experiment

Compare a dry twig, a piece of cardboard, and a wooden table. Keep the sensor position and gain similar. Which surface carries the clearest vibration? Record your observations, and label them as your own test sounds—not insect calls.

Care and safety: Do not pierce, cut, or tightly clamp living plants, disturb insects, or attach the sensor to an animal. Keep electronics dry. Never connect bare piezo wires to an unknown port or speaker output; use a compatible input or preamp.

What will it sound like?

A piezo sensor may capture knocks, rubbing, movement, and other structure-borne vibrations. It will not automatically identify an insect, and it does not guarantee better results in every setting. Background handling noise can be louder than the signal you want.

Curious minds ask…

Can insects really make sounds?

Yes. Depending on species, sounds can be made by rubbing body parts together, wing movement, drumming, or other mechanisms. Many arthropods also sense vibrations rather than airborne sound.

Why use real recordings?

Real recordings preserve the texture and variation of animal behavior. Synthetic tones test the exhibit but should never be presented as a species' actual call.

Can we add live insect sensors later?

Yes. A future version could connect a contact microphone, piezo sensor, or ultrasonic detector to a small computer and stream authorized sensor output to this page.
